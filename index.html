mta-audit

Reliability testing for multi-touch attribution

Attribution models always hand you an answer. Almost none tell you whether that answer would survive a small change in assumptions. mta-audit checks exactly that, and says so plainly when the data can't support a call.

Problem
Campaign rankings move with choices nobody reports: the conversion window, how identities get stitched, which touchpoints went missing, which model you picked. Teams act on rankings that may not hold.
Approach
Journey-level bootstrap (whole journeys, never rows), conversion-window and delayed-converter checks, first/last/linear/Markov disagreement, and identity and touchpoint-loss simulations. Score weights are published as policy, with a sensitivity report.
Benchmark
On an 8,000-user sample of the public Criteo attribution dataset, injected duplicate events (1–10%) were caught every time and dropped the data-quality score from 72 to about 50.
Limits
It measures stability, not causal lift. It doesn't replace an incrementality test.
Python 3.11–3.13BootstrapMarkov attributionCriteo datasetCI

Why install it today: if a budget decision is riding on an attribution report, one command tells you in minutes whether those campaign rankings would survive a different conversion window or a bad identity match, before anyone moves money.

silentfail-bench

Auditing the winner: critic disagreement adds no detectable failure signal beyond the selector

A coding agent tries a task several times and a scoring model picks its best attempt. A third to a half of those picks are broken: the agent said it was finished, the scorer agreed, the tests disagreed. The proposed safety net is to ask a separate panel of scoring models to grade the same attempt and treat their disagreement as a warning. I pre-registered that test and ran it on two benchmarks. Disagreement doesn't work, and I can now say how much it doesn't by.

Study
Two benchmarks with the projects' real test suites as ground truth: a pilot on SWE-bench Verified and a pre-registered replication on SWE-bench Multilingual. 46 repositories analysed, nine languages, 47 confirmed silent failures. Reward models from 1B to 32B, three frontier judges, four context lengths, windowed auditing, two selection regimes. Every analysis was declared before its data existed.
The answer
On the endpoint the proposal registered, adding panel disagreement to the selector's own score buys nothing. I computed it four ways; the estimates disagree in sign and every interval contains zero. The most I can defend as an upper bound on disagreement's value is 0.12 AUROC, and that's the loosest of the estimators that use all the data.
What the control showed
A result I had assumed and had to give up: with this selector, the committed attempt fails no more often than one it passed over, so I claim a silent-failure rate, not concentration. What selection does do is consume the selector's own signal. The same score ranks failures at 0.81 among attempts it rejected and 0.61 among the ones it committed, which is the gap the design was built around, measured directly.
What didn't travel
Two point estimates cleared their bar on the small benchmark and died on the large one. A windowed variant fell from 0.686 to chance, and so did patch size, the model-free baseline that had outranked every model I ran. Both were regularities of twelve repositories, not facts about agents.
Where the signal is
Not in disagreement. A frontier judge reading the diff on its own ranks silent failures at about two-thirds, and the critics turned out to be stable instruments, not noisy ones. Averaging how much they differ throws away the signal each one carries.
Now
An 8-page submission cut and a 24-page extended version, both with the pre-registration trail and six documented instrument failures that would each have produced a false finding. The open decision is whether to submit or first run the one missing experiment, a selector strong enough to rank failures before selection. That one is written up with its bars fixed and deliberately not run. Nothing is published.

IMA

Integrated marketing attribution, anchored in MMM

Mix models see the whole budget but only at channel level. Attribution sees individual paths but breaks down as privacy rules take identifiers away. The IMA framework, from a paper by Bhat et al., ties the two together: Bayesian attribution that gives campaign- and placement-level reads that stay consistent with the MMM, without user-level tracking.

Source
I'm implementing the framework from “Integrated Marketing Attribution: A Bayesian Framework for Privacy-Safe Granular Measurement Anchored in MMM” (Bhat et al., arXiv:2606.16878). I didn't write the paper. My part is turning it into working code.
Why it's hard
The granular estimates have to add up to what the MMM says. Small campaigns need priors that help without drowning out the data. And the output has to be checkable by people who will never read the model.
Note
This is work from my current role, so there's no code or data here. I'm glad to walk through the method and the design choices in an interview.

SIGNAL

An AI investment research system

A research tool that pulls together financial data, a quantitative model, outside evidence, and LLM reasoning, so you can follow an investment thesis without wading through all of it yourself. It's early. The design choices are the interesting part so far.

Design
A multi-agent workflow: separate agents gather evidence, argue the bull and bear cases, and pull them into one view.
Key choice
The quant score is computed on its own, apart from the LLM's reasoning, so the model can't talk itself into a number. When the two disagree, that disagreement is shown, not hidden.
Evals
Tracing and evaluation with Langfuse from the start, so I can check whether conclusions are backed by the sources they cite.
Status
In development. The interface prototype is live below; the research agents behind it aren't public yet.
